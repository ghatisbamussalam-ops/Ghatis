When the Audience Sang in Arabic

What a moment at Cheb Khaled’s Paris concert can tell us about language, belonging and music as a historical source.

Cultural essayمقال ثقافيAbout 9 minutesنحو 9 دقائق للقراءةWritten in Englishمكتوب بالإنجليزية

ملخص بالعربية في سبتمبر 1998، وفي قاعة بيرسي بباريس، توقف الشاب خالد في منتصف سطر عربي من أغنية «عائشة»، وأدار الميكروفون إلى الجمهور، فأكمل الآلاف السطر. يقرأ المقال هذه اللحظة القصيرة بوصفها مصدرًا تاريخيًا، ويفرّق بين ثلاثة أشياء: قصد المغنّي، ودور حركته في العرض، وأثرها الاجتماعي الأوسع، ثم يضع الفيديو إلى جانب ما كتبته الصحافة في ذلك الوقت عن الراي والشباب واللغة العربية في فرنسا. لا يدّعي المقال أن الغناء بالعربية كان فعل مقاومة، ولا أن القاعة كلها أرادت قول شيء واحد. يرى فقط أن العربية كانت في الأغنية منذ البداية، وأنها صارت صوتًا علنيًا في لحظة تركها فيها المغنّي للجمهور.

A singer stops in the middle of a line, turns to the crowd and points the microphone at them. Thousands of voices finish it for him.

That happened at Bercy in Paris in September 1998, during Cheb Khaled’s performance of “Aïcha” at the concert “1, 2, 3 Soleils”. The audience had been singing from the first bars. What changed was the moment the song reached its Arabic section. Khaled sings, “Nabghi Aïcha wa nmout ali…”, leaves the line unfinished and brings the microphone to the crowd. The hall completes it, clearly.

I want to read that moment as a historical source, and to be exact about how much it can carry. Three things need to be kept apart.

IntentionWhat Khaled meant by it. Out of reach: a gesture and a face cannot show what someone was thinking, and nothing suggests the concert was planned as a political statement.
RoleWhat the gesture did in the show. Visible: he makes room for the audience’s voice.
EffectWhat it meant socially. We can only approach this by setting the video beside what was written at the time about rai, young people and Arabic in France.

That is the method of this essay, and its limits apply to everything below.

I come to this as someone who studies Yemeni song, where the audience is often part of the recording: a listener repeats a line, corrects it, comments, asks the singer to go back. Those moments are easy to hear as noise. They are also evidence of what the song was for the people in the room. Bercy is a very different stage, but the question carries over. What does it mean when the listeners, and not the singer, carry the line?

From the margins to Bercy

“1, 2, 3 Soleils” was more than a concert by three of rai’s best-known voices. It came as rai was moving out of cafés and small clubs and into French media and the music business. On 27 September 1998, Le Monde described the move from small venues in Paris, Lyon and Marseille to a larger stage. Two days later it called the concert a meeting of Arabic music and Western technology: a big production, new arrangements, many musicians and clear commercial aims, including a live recording and broadcast.

The context matters because the concert was not outside the industry. It was a major commercial and media event. It also brought together people with very different ties to North Africa, to Arabic and to rai. A concert can be a successful product and still be a place where social meanings appear that marketing does not explain.

An audience that knew the song, if not every word

In her report for Le Monde on 29 September 1998, Véronique Mortaigne noted that many young people held on to Arabic even when they did not speak it well. She described the language as a mark of cultural difference and a link to holidays in the family’s country of origin and to life at home. This was written at the time. It is not an idea added to the event years later.

I do not take it to mean that everyone in the hall had North African roots, or that I can read a listener’s story from their voice. The report places the shared singing in a wider setting, where language meant more than understanding every word. For some listeners Arabic was part of daily life. Others had it from home or from songs, or as a family and emotional tie they could not fully speak.

When an audience sings a song it knows, it does more than show it has learned the words. It makes shared knowledge audible in public. Something that belongs to home, memory or a recording is heard in a large hall in Paris. Not everyone felt the same thing. But the scene raises a plain question: what does it mean for a group to sing together in a language that may not be its first, or the one it uses most in public?

The moment the balance of voices changes

The audience was already singing before Khaled and Faudel began, and it kept going with them. So the point is not that Khaled discovered the crowd knew the song. The change comes in the Arabic section. He stops mid-line, turns, and the crowd takes it.

For a few seconds the singer who leads the show hands the main voice to the audience. Giving a line to the crowd is an ordinary move in live music. What makes this one worth reading is its timing. It comes as the song moves into Arabic, and reports from the same year describe Arabic as something many young listeners in France kept without fully speaking it. I do not need to say Khaled meant it as a message. It is enough to notice that the Arabic section became the place where the audience’s voice stood out.

Khaled between “French” and “Algerian”

The picture gets more complicated with something Khaled said about himself. An RFI Musique article published shortly before the concert quotes an earlier interview with the French magazine Le Nouvel Observateur, from December 1997. Khaled objected to being introduced in some countries as a French singer: “Pourtant je ne suis pas français, je suis algérien” (“But I am not French, I am Algerian”).

This is not a key to every gesture on stage, and it should not shrink him to a choice between two countries. It does show that the label “French music” was never neutral. An artist can work inside the French music industry, play its biggest stages and reach very different listeners, and still describe himself through another identity. The two need not conflict, though the way an artist is introduced and sold can make them look like rivals.

The audience should not be reduced to one identity either. Bercy was not a single group. It held different generations, backgrounds, languages and hopes. The song gave them a shared moment, and the moment could mean something different to each person.

A song does not cross borders empty

Work on migration and music helps here. In “Migration and Music” (2020), Martin Stokes argues that migrant music should not be treated as a fixed culture, or as a simple move from tradition to modern life. Music is not only a mirror of an identity that already exists. It is something people do together, and through it they build and argue over their ties and their sense of belonging.

On that view, a song does not travel from Algeria to France like a product with no history. It enters radio, a concert hall, a family home, a car, the memory of a listener who may not follow every word. In a new setting it can become a sign of something that was less visible before, without losing what it meant earlier. It gains layers.

Annette Weiner’s Inalienable Possessions (1992) adds a further question, with a caveat: her argument comes from specific fieldwork, and “Aïcha” is not a direct example of it. Weiner looks at things that can be given or passed on while staying tied to a group, a history or a place. Applied loosely, her idea asks whether something cultural can reach a new audience without shedding its links to memory, community or place. Songs change through recordings, arrangements and live shows, and they can enter a global market. Sharing them does not always cut those links.

One more detail near the end of the concert. The instruments stop together, and then the oud plays alone, taking up a melody from the song. I would not turn the oud into a political symbol or call it a return to an original culture. What I can say is simpler: the melody carries on in a different sound after the others have stopped, and familiar material is heard in a new arrangement. It is a musical detail first and a cultural image second. It still reminds me that when music travels, one sound does not simply replace another. Sounds, and the relations between them, get rearranged.

Can a concert be a historical source?

I think this one deserves to be read as one. Not because it settles who the audience was, but because it keeps details that artists’ public statements tend to miss: who starts singing, when the singer steps back, when the crowd comes forward, which language lets the shared voice stand out, and how the song changes in a new arrangement.

The video cannot replace press archives or historical research, but it can join them as evidence. A recording keeps gestures, timing and the sound of a crowd that a newspaper cannot fully describe. Reports from the time supply context the video cannot explain by itself. Together they give a reading richer than either alone, and one that stays open to question.

I am not trying to prove that singing in Arabic was an act of resistance, or that the whole hall wanted to say one thing. The point is smaller and, I think, more interesting. Arabic was part of the song from the start. It became audible as a public voice at one moment because the singer let the audience carry it. Something that may have lived in people’s memories was sung out loud. In the short space between the singer’s voice and the crowd’s, I can see something of the social history of migration and belonging, as something people did and not something they declared.

Sometimes social history does not show up in what an artist says. It shows up when something that was there all along, a language, a memory, a sense of belonging, is suddenly heard differently.
